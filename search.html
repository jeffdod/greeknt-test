    How this page works

    Reading the notes

    Punctuation and spacing in compound words are not recorded in the apparatus, and accents and a movable nu or final sigma are recorded only where a reading shows them. The Greek under each note is the text of this edition, verse by verse. Point at a Greek word, or tap it, to see its Strong’s number, dictionary form, gloss, and parsing. The underlining was placed by software; a note about words the main text does not contain, such as a verse it omits, shows the verse without an underline. Where the main text of a note has an ellipsis (…), the words quoted are underlined, and if its readings do not all have an ellipsis too, the words in between are underlined with a lighter line, so that the whole stretch the note covers can be seen. The English is the Boyd ASVBT, a form of the American Standard Version made to follow the Byzantine text, which is in the public domain; it is matched verse for verse to this edition, so a verse number this edition prints without text has no English. An English search looks in the verses a note covers, not in the wording of the note itself. “Effect on the English” comes from an English translation of this Greek text, whose footnotes mark the places where a variant changes the translation. A note is marked “Changes the English” when that translation has a footnote at the same place; every other note is marked “No change to the English.” The marks reflect that translator’s judgment, not a measurement, and a few variants may matter to the English more or less than the translator thought. The downloaded spreadsheet has a last column, “Changes the English,” with Yes or No for each note.

    Kinds of difference

    The kind of difference is worked out automatically by comparing the words of the main text and the reading, and every reading has one main kind, which may be joined by the note “has bracketed text.” A reading “reads the same as the base text” when it has the same words in the same order, or “only the brackets differ” when it has the same words in the same order and some of them are in brackets. Bracketed words or letters can also appear in a reading of any other kind, such as a different word order or an added word; the Kind filter “Has bracketed text” finds all of these, and the reading shows the note after its kind. It has a “different spelling” when the words correspond one for one but at least one is spelled differently, and “different word order” when it has the same words rearranged. It “omits the base reading” when it leaves out all of the main-text words, “omits words” when it leaves out some of them, in one place or several, with the rest in the same order, and “adds words” when it adds words in one place or several, with the main-text words in the same order. Any other reading “replaces the base text.” “Elision” and “crasis” are used when that is the only difference, as in αλλ’ for αλλα, or κακει for και εκει. A reading that is only a bracketed list of editions is “single brackets,” “double brackets,” or “chevrons around reading,” and a few readings that are described in English are labeled “Other.” Two words are the same word when they have the same Strong’s number from the Robinson-Pierpont text and the same part of speech and are spelled alike. A word with the same Strong’s number and parsing but a different spelling is a spelling difference. A movable nu or final sigma is treated as the same word only where the reading marks it in parentheses, as in εστι(ν). Accents and breathings are compared only when both words show them. A word that has no analysis in the Robinson-Pierpont data counts as the same word in a different spelling when it differs only in a way that old spellings often do, such as ει for ι. “Shortens the text” and “Lengthens the text” are counted by words alone: a reading with fewer words than the main text shortens it, and one with more words lengthens it, whatever its kind.

    Word comparison

    “Show comparison” under a reading lines its words up against the main text, with each word’s dictionary form, Strong’s number, gloss and parsing. Each number is the place of a word in the main text, and its partner in the reading carries the same number; shading shows the same pairing, so either one is enough on its own. A plus sign marks an added word, and a dashed outline marks a word that is added or omitted. A reading of up to six words shows every word with its details. For a longer reading, point at a word, or select it, to see the details below; when a word and its partner are not identical, both are shown and the differences are marked. In a longer reading, words that move together share one shade, a word that is spelled or parsed differently has a shade of its own, and unchanged words in place are left plain. When a word and its partner are spelled differently, the fewest letters that differ are highlighted and underlined with a wavy line: if you took the marked letters out of both words, the letters left would be the same letters in the same order. A word that only has an extra letter shows the mark on the longer word alone. A full cell also says “spelled differently.” A movable nu or final sigma that the reading puts in parentheses does not count as a difference. The pairing is worked out by software from Strong’s numbers and spellings, as for the kinds of difference, and a word with no analysis in the Robinson-Pierpont data is shown without one.

    The spreadsheet download

    The CSV download contains every matching note, not only the ones shown on the page, with one row for each reading, one row for each case where an edition prints the main-text reading in brackets, and one row where ECM prints a split guiding line. When a note is also one of the fifty variants, the last column of its rows lists the manuscripts cited for each reading, with the readings separated by ‖, with raised marks written as superscript characters (for example 399ᶜᵛⁱᵈ).
